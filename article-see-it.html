See It. Solve It. Sorted.

How I cut a 45-minute daily report down to 10 — and freed time for the work that matters.

Every PMO has at least one task that's been done the same way for years. The kind of task nobody questions because that's just how it's done. Mine was a daily Concessions and Permits status report.

In aerospace, Concessions and Permits are the formal documents raised when something doesn't conform to design. A part might be slightly outside tolerance. A process step might need to be deviated from. Either way, the deviation can't just happen quietly — every one of these requires formal authorisation, with full traceability across multiple functions. At any given time there are dozens of these live across active programmes, each at a different stage of review, approval, or closure.

The daily report existed so that everyone knew where each one stood. What was open. What was approved. What was holding production up. Reasonable purpose, useful audience. The problem was how it was built.

The problem I inherited

When I joined the PMO, my first task each morning was to produce this report. Manually. I'd open each project's folder, count the PDFs, read through emails for status updates, stitch the data together in a spreadsheet, format it, then send it out. Thirty to forty-five minutes, every single morning, before I could begin any actual work.

The real cost wasn't even the time it took me. It was the downstream effect. Several teams were waiting on this report to plan their day. If I was late, they were late. The bottleneck wasn't in production — it was in the reporting that production depended on.

It was a textbook example of work that exists because nobody has questioned it lately.

The fix had two parts

The first instinct was to automate everything and delete the manual process. That instinct was wrong. The data we had served two different purposes, and each needed its own tool.

The automated tracker. I built an Excel macro that reads each project's folder and counts the live PDFs. That gave us real-time status of every concession and permit without anyone having to manually update anything. Open a file. Refresh. See where things stand.

The manual tracker. The existing spreadsheet stayed in place. It held over two years of historical data — when each item was raised, when it was closed, who owned it, how long it took. That history is essential for any kind of trend analysis or learning.

The automated tracker tells you where things are right now. The manual one tells you how things have been over time. Each one had a job the other couldn't do, so keeping both was deliberate — not a compromise.

Why Power BI, not just Excel

Initially, the visuals lived inside the manual Excel tracker. Raised versus closed counts. Average turnaround time. Ageing analysis. A burndown chart for items open more than two weeks. It worked. But I could see the writing on the wall.

Excel has a ceiling. Each new entry made the file slower. Every refresh carried risk of corruption. Two years of historical data sitting alongside dozens of formulas and pivot tables meant we were one bad save away from losing it all. The longer this went on, the bigger the risk would get.

So I separated concerns. Excel got stripped back to what Excel is genuinely good at: data entry and storage. Power BI took over visualisation, reporting, and sharing. I rebuilt every visual cleanly in Power BI and pointed it directly at the source files. Now anyone can open the dashboard, see the current status and historical trends, and do it without ever touching the underlying data.

Excel holds the data. Power BI tells the story.

What changed

The headline number is that my daily reporting effort dropped from 30–45 minutes down to 10–15. A 66% reduction. That's a real number and worth quoting, but honestly, the time saving was the smallest win.

The bigger win was that multiple teams now self-serve from the same dashboard. They don't have to wait for my report. They don't have to email me asking for clarification. The information is there, live, when they need it.

And critically, the time I've freed up hasn't disappeared into more reports. It's gone into forward planning, risk conversations, and stakeholder alignment. The work that actually moves programmes forward, rather than the work that documents whether they have.

What this taught me

Looking back, the lesson wasn't really about Power BI or Excel macros. The lesson was about noticing.

Every PMO inherits processes that exist because someone, once, decided that's how it should work. Most of those processes are fine. Some of them have quietly become bottlenecks, and the longer they sit unchallenged, the harder they are to question. The instinct I try to bring to every new task is to ask: does this need to be done this way, or has nobody questioned it lately?

That question is portable. The industry changes. The data sources change. The principle doesn't. Find the friction. Design the fix. Free up the team to do higher-value work.

That, more than the dashboard itself, is what I'd want any future programme to remember from this story.
