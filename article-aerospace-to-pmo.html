From Aerospace Engineering to PMO

What transferred, what didn't, and why I stopped worrying about the gap on paper.

I graduated with a degree in Aerospace Engineering. Within a couple of years, I was working in a PMO. On paper, that looks like a pivot. In practice, it was less of a jump than it sounds.

When people see "Aerospace Engineering" on a CV next to "Project Management Officer", the natural question is whether the technical degree was wasted, or whether the PMO role is a fallback. Neither is true — and unpacking why is, I think, useful for anyone navigating a similar transition.

What an engineering degree actually teaches you

Most aerospace engineering modules aren't about aircraft. They're about thinking systematically. Aerodynamics, propulsion, structures, materials, control systems — each one is a small lesson in the same underlying discipline: break a complex problem into manageable parts, model the parts well enough to be useful, and accept that the model is never the territory.

You also learn to work with documentation that has to be right the first time. Engineering drawings, calculations, test reports — they're not narrative documents. They're traceable. Someone three years from now should be able to read what you wrote and understand exactly why you decided what you decided.

That instinct for traceability turned out to be the single most useful thing I brought with me.

What PMO actually involves

A PMO role isn't administrative. Or rather, the administrative parts are the visible tip of something larger.

The real job is keeping a programme honest. Scope, schedule, risk, change, dependency — these are all just structured ways of asking the same question: are we still on track to deliver what we said we'd deliver, and if not, what are we doing about it?

The PMO is the part of the team that says yes, but and what about. Not to slow things down — but to make sure that progress is real and not just felt.

What transferred

Several things turned out to be more useful than I expected:

*Comfort with ambiguity in technical detail.* Programme managers often need someone in the room who can read a technical document, ask the right question, and not be intimidated by jargon. An engineering background means I don't switch off when an engineer starts explaining something.

*The instinct to verify before reporting.* In engineering, an unverified calculation is a liability. The same applies to PMO data. If I'm pulling numbers for a steer committee, I want to know where they came from, how stale they are, and whether the assumptions still hold. That discipline came from coursework, not from PMO training.

*Tolerance for documentation.* Many people find programme documentation tedious. Engineers tend not to — or at least, we have a higher tolerance for it because we've been doing it since first year.

*Working backwards from constraints.* Engineering problems often start with "given these constraints, design something that works". Programme planning is structurally the same. Given a deadline, a budget, and a set of dependencies, what's the actual achievable plan?

What didn't transfer

Some things had to be unlearned, or at least adapted:

*The temptation to over-engineer.* Engineering rewards rigour. PMO rewards proportional rigour. Spending three hours perfecting a report that needed to be good enough in twenty minutes is a habit I had to actively break.

*Solo problem-solving.* A lot of engineering coursework is individual. A lot of PMO work is collaborative. The skill of knowing when to ask, who to ask, and what to ask — that took longer to build.

*Comfort with uncertainty in people.* Engineering models can be wrong, but they don't change their minds. People do. Programme work is mostly about people, and that took a different muscle.

What I'd tell someone making the same move

The thing nobody tells you is that the "career change" framing is largely an artefact of how we write CVs. In reality, the underlying skills — analytical thinking, attention to detail, structured communication, comfort with complexity — transfer fluidly between technical and programme roles.

What matters less is the degree label. What matters more is whether you can sit in a meeting with engineers, finance, operations, and quality, follow all four conversations, and synthesise them into something a director can act on.

If you can do that, your degree doesn't need to match your job title. It just needs to have taught you how to think.
